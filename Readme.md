# European Put vs. Floating-Strike Lookback Put  Monte-Carlo under Black–Scholes

Pricing an at-the-money European put and a floating-strike lookback put in the
Black–Scholes model, by closed-form formula and by Monte-Carlo simulation, in
order to isolate **where simulation error comes from** in each case.

The short version of the result: for the European put the Monte-Carlo error is
purely statistical and vanishes as $1/\sqrt{N}$. For the lookback put it is
**not** statistical  it is a systematic discretization bias that does not
improve with more paths, only with a finer time grid.

*Coursework for ACTU-F4002 (Modèles financiers II), M.Sc. Actuarial Science,
Université Libre de Bruxelles.*

---

## Setup

Common parameters for both options:

$$S_0 = 110,\quad K = 110,\quad r = 4\%,\quad \sigma = 15\%,\quad T = 0.5$$

Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$ the underlying follows
$dS(t) = r\,S(t)\,dt + \sigma S(t)\,dW(t)$.

**Payoffs.** European put: $(K - S(T))_+$. Floating-strike lookback put:
$M^S(T) - S(T)$ with $M^S(T) = \max_{0 \le s \le T} S(s)$ — the realised
drawdown from the running maximum. The $\max(\cdot, 0)$ is redundant there,
since $M^S(T) \ge S(T)$ almost surely.

## Method

Paths are generated by the **exact log-discretization** of the GBM, not an
Euler scheme:

$$S_i = S_{i-1}\exp\Big(\big(r - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\big)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon_i\Big),\qquad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

This matters for the argument. The simulated $S_i$ follow the exact law of the
continuous GBM *at the grid instants*, so the time step introduces no error in
the marginal distribution. Any remaining step-dependence therefore cannot be
blamed on the path scheme  it has to come from the payoff functional itself.

Prices are the mean of discounted payoffs, with the usual CLT confidence
interval at $1-\alpha = 95\%$:

$$\Big[\hat\pi_{\mathrm{MC}} - 1.96\,\hat s_Y/\sqrt{N}\;;\; \hat\pi_{\mathrm{MC}} + 1.96\,\hat s_Y/\sqrt{N}\Big]$$

Closed-form benchmarks: the Black–Scholes put for Q1, and the floating-strike
lookback put formula for Q2.

## Q1 - European put: error is statistical only

Monte-Carlo at $N = 50{,}000$, weekly step ($n = 26$): **3.6124**.
Black–Scholes closed form: **3.6084**. Absolute gap 0.0040, i.e. **0.11%**, and
the theoretical price lies inside the 95% interval $[3.602,\ 3.623]$.

| $N$ | MC price | Relative error (%) | Standard error |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | 3.5892 | 0.53 | 0.023 |
| 5,000 | 3.6015 | 0.19 | 0.011 |
| 10,000 | 3.6058 | 0.07 | 0.008 |
| 50,000 | 3.6124 | 0.11 | 0.004 |
| 100,000 | 3.6093 | 0.02 | 0.003 |
| 500,000 | 3.6087 | 0.01 | 0.001 |

Weekly ($n = 26$) and daily ($n = 180$) steps are statistically
indistinguishable. The estimator is unbiased; only $N$ matters.

## Q2 - Lookback put: error is a systematic bias

Same machinery, different behaviour. At fixed $N = 100{,}000$, varying only the
number of steps (reference closed-form price $LP_{\mathrm{ref}} = 8.4928$):

| $n$ | $\Delta t$ | MC price | Standard error | Relative error (%) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12 | 0.04167 | 6.6318 | 0.019 | 21.91 |
| 26 | 0.01923 | 7.1308 | 0.019 | 16.04 |
| 52 | 0.00962 | 7.5237 | 0.020 | 11.41 |
| 180 | 0.00278 | 7.9731 | 0.020 | 6.12 |
| 360 | 0.00139 | 8.1020 | 0.019 | 4.60 |
| 720 | 0.00069 | 8.2368 | 0.020 | 3.01 |

Three observations, and together they identify the error:

1. **The relative error does not fall with $N$.** At fixed $n$ it is essentially
   constant across $N \in \{10^3, \dots, 5\times10^5\}$. Statistical noise cannot
   behave this way.

2. **The gap is far larger than the noise.** At $(n, N) = (180,\ 10^5)$,
   $\widehat{LP} = 7.9591$ with $\hat\sigma = 0.0195$ and
   $IC_{95} = [7.9210,\ 7.9973]$. The distance to the closed form is
   $8.4928 - 7.9591 = 0.5337$, which is $0.5337/0.0195 \approx 27$ standard
   errors. A 27-sigma discrepancy is not a fluctuation.

3. **The error scales with $\Delta t$, at an identifiable order.** Dividing
   $\Delta t$ by 60 ($n: 12 \to 720$) divides the error by
   $\approx 7 \approx \sqrt{60}$  consistent with a bias of order
   $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t})$.

Stability was confirmed over 30 independent replications at each step size: the
spread *within* a step size is an order of magnitude below the spread *between*
step sizes, so the residual is bias and not noise.

**Why.** The discrete maximum $\max_{0\le k\le n} S(t_k)$ is almost surely below
the continuous maximum $M^S(T)$ a path can peak strictly between two
observation instants, and the grid never sees that peak. The estimator is
therefore biased downward by construction, and the confidence interval above
quantifies the *statistical* uncertainty only; it does not cover this bias.

Retained configuration: $(n, N) = (180,\ 100{,}000)$ daily observation
frequency, standard error under 0.25% of the estimated price, and sharply
diminishing returns from further refinement.

## Economic comparison

| Option | Price (EUR) | Ratio to $P_{\mathrm{BS}}$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| European put, Black–Scholes ($K = 110$) | 3.6084 | 1.00 |
| Lookback put, Monte-Carlo ($n=180$, $N=10^5$) | 7.9591 | 2.21 |
| Lookback put, closed form | 8.4928 | 2.35 |

The lookback costs about 2.35× the at-the-money European put. The European put
pays only if $S(T) < K$, so its protection depends on a threshold fixed in
advance; the lookback pays the drawdown from the best level actually reached,
removing the timing decision entirely. The wider protection is what the premium
buys.
